Connection-Based Aloha: Modeling, Optimization,
and Effects of Connection Establishment
Xinran Zhao
and Lin Dai , Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract— According to whether a connection to the receiver
is established before data transmission, random access schemes
can be divided into two categories: connection-based and
connection-free. For cellular networks, its connection-based
access mechanism has been heavily criticized for being ill-
suited to Machine-to-Machine (M2M) communications. With the
existing studies overwhelmingly focusing on the physical-layer
transmission and detection design of various connection-free
access schemes, less attention has been paid to the fundamen-
tal understanding of when connection establishment could be
beneficial. The challenge originates from the lack of proper
modeling of connection-based random access. In this paper,
a scalable model that incorporates each node’s queueing behavior
is proposed for connection-based slotted Aloha, based on which
both the data throughput and the mean queueing delay of
data packets are characterized, optimized, and compared with
those for connection-free slotted Aloha. Conditions for beneficial
connection establishment in terms of the optimal throughput
and queueing delay performance are discussed and applied to
cellular networks with the newly introduced Random Access-
based Small Data Transmission (RA-SDT) schemes. The analysis
offers important insights for the optimal access design of M2M
communications.

I. INTRODUCTION
T
HE rapid proliferation of Machine-Type Devices (MTDs)
is yielding a paradigm shift from the traditional Human-
to-Human (H2H) communications to Machine-to-Machine
(M2M) communications [1]. A central challenge brought by
M2M communications is to support massive access of MTDs
in an efficient and timely manner. As a fundamental type of
multiple access, random access, with which nodes indepen-
dently decide when to transmit in a distributed fashion, is an
appealing solution to M2M communications by virtue of its
simplicity and scalability.
Numerous random access protocols have been proposed
since Abramson’s pioneering work on Aloha [2]. According

transmission, they can be broadly divided into two cat-
egories: connection-free and connection-based. Specifically,
with connection-free random access schemes, nodes directly
transmit their data packets. When there are concurrent trans-
missions from multiple nodes, transmission failures may occur,
and the resultant wasted time is determined by the length of
a data packet. For connection-based random access, on the
other hand, each node first sends a request, and transmits
its data packets only after receiving the acknowledgement
from the receiver that indicates the successful detection of its
request, i.e., a connection is established. As a consequence,
only requests are involved in transmission failures, and data
packets can be transmitted over the reserved resources.
Intuitively, when the data packet length is much larger
than the request length, connection-based random access is
preferred as the benefit brought by the reduction of trans-
mission failure time would outweigh the extra overhead for
connection establishment. Nevertheless, connection establish-
ment may become costly for short-packet transmissions, which
is common in M2M communications. Such concerns have
long been raised over the Long Term Evolution (LTE) cellular
systems, where its grant-based (i.e., connection-based) access
mechanism has been intensely criticized for the low efficiency
and long delay caused by the connection-establishment pro-
cedure [3]. To this end, there have been extensive discussions
on the suitability of grant-based random access schemes for
M2M communications, with many advocating that grant-free
(i.e., connection-free) random access is more favorable [4],
[5], [6]. Most of the studies have focused on improving the
success probability of uplink data transmission in grant-free
random access by designing various transmission schemes for
collision reduction [7], [8], [9], [10] and detection algorithms
that exploit the sparsity nature of MTDs’ activities [11], [12],
[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19].
Note that the advanced data transmission and detection
schemes proposed for grant-free random access can also be
applied to grant-based random access to reduce the request
transmission failures. Given the same physical-layer design,
from the perspective of medium access control layer, whether
a grant-free random access protocol outperforms a grant-based
one is determined by the data payload length and protocol
overhead. Specifically, for given overhead parameters, there
exists a critical threshold of data payload length, only above
which connection establishment is beneficial. Such a threshold
was recently characterized in [20] for both Aloha and Carrier
Sense Multiple Access (CSMA) in terms of the maximum data
throughput. The analysis shows that for Aloha, the threshold

There are, nevertheless, a few limitations when applying the
above results to practice. First of all, the optimal throughput
performance was considered in [20], to achieve which nodes’
queues should be pushed to saturation, i.e., the queues are
always busy. When the queueing delay performance becomes
the main concern, however, the focus should be shifted to the
unsaturated condition, only under which the mean queueing
delay of data packets is bounded. More importantly, in [20],
a channel-centric model was adopted, with which the through-
put analysis can be greatly simplified by only focusing on the
aggregate traffic of nodes instead of each individual node’s
behavior. Yet by excluding nodes’ queues from the model, it is
difficult to further characterize the queueing performance and
obtain the optimal setting of access parameters of nodes, which
is of paramount importance for practical network control.
With nodes’ queues taken into consideration, a node-centric
model was developed in [21] for connection-free Aloha, where
the coupled service rates of nodes’ queues were derived
by characterizing the state transition of Head-of-Line (HOL)
packets in each queue and establishing the fixed-point equa-
tions of the steady-state probability of successful transmission
of HOL packets in saturated and unsaturated conditions.
The model in [21], however, cannot be directly applied to
connection-based Aloha because instead of contending for
channel access individually, with connection-based Aloha,
data packets are transmitted in a batch after a connection
is established. That calls for a novel node-centric model for
connection-based Aloha, based on which both throughput and
queueing delay performance can be characterized, optimized,
and further compared with that of connection-free Aloha.
Note that for connection-based random access, the requests,
rather than data packets, contend with each other. Therefore,
the key to performance analysis lies in proper modeling
of the behavior of each node’s requests. In
this paper,
a request-queue model is proposed for throughput and delay
optimization of connection-based slotted Aloha. Specifically,
by introducing a request queue for each node, both the data
throughput and the mean queueing delay of data packets are
derived based on the service time distribution of the request
queue. The optimal transmission probability of nodes for max-
imizing the data throughput or minimizing the mean queueing
delay is also obtained as an explicit function of the number
of nodes or the data input rate of each node. The optimal
throughput and delay performance of connection-based slotted
Aloha is further compared with that of connection-free slotted
Aloha, based on which the criteria for beneficial connection
establishment are discussed. The analysis is also applied to
the Fifth-Generation (5G) cellular networks for performance
comparison of the recently introduced grant-free 2-step Ran-
dom Access-based Small Data Transmission (RA-SDT) and
grant-based 4-step RA-SDT schemes [22].
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Related
work is reviewed in Section II. Section III describes our system
model of connection-based slotted Aloha. In Section IV, the
service process of each request queue is characterized, based
on which the maximum data throughput and the minimum
mean queueing delay are derived and verified by simula-
tion results in Section V. The comparative analysis is con-
ducted between connection-free slotted Aloha and connection-
based slotted Aloha and further applied to 5G networks
in Section VI. Possible extensions of the analysis are dis-
cussed in Section VII. Finally, conclusions are summarized
in Section VIII.
II. RELATED WORK
In this section, we will review the previous studies on mod-
eling and performance analysis of connection-based Aloha.
Similar to connection-free Aloha, models for connection-
based Aloha can be in general categorized into channel-centric
and node-centric, with the former focusing on the aggregate
requests and the latter on each node’s queueing behavior.
Early studies adopted channel-centric models to analyze the
throughput and access delay performance by either assuming
a network with an infinite number of nodes [23], [24], or a
finite-node network with each node equipped of a one-packet
buffer [25], [26], [27]. The number of aggregate requests of
nodes is usually assumed as Poisson distributed [23], [24],
or characterized based on the state transition of the number
of backlogged nodes (i.e., nodes with nonempty buffer) [25],
[26], [27]. The proposed channel-centric models have been
widely adopted in later studies on modeling and performance
optimization for connection-based access schemes in LTE and
5G cellular systems [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33]. When
modeling a cellular network with massive MTDs, for instance,
the number of requests per time slot is often assumed to follow
a Poisson distribution [28], [29]. However, by ignoring each
node’s data queue, the adaptive tuning of access parameters,
such as the transmission probability of each node, can only be
performed based on the time-varying network status such as
the number of backlogged nodes [30], [31], [32], [33], which
in practice is difficult to track and estimate accurately, rather
than the data input rate of each node.
To include nodes’ queues, various node-centric models
have been proposed in [34], [35], and [36]. In [34], the
joint distribution of nodes’ queue lengths was obtained for a
two-node connection-based slotted Aloha network, which is,
nevertheless, difficult to be generalized to an n-node network
due to high modeling complexity. Therefore, the symmetric
scenario, where nodes have identical data input rates and
transmission probabilities, has been considered in [35] and
[36], where the modeling dimensions can be greatly reduced
by focusing on each single queue. Specifically, in [35], each
node’s data buffer was modeled as a Geo/D/1 queue with
vacation. By assuming that the number of aggregate requests
in each time slot is Poisson distributed with the average rate
G, the mean queueing delay of data packets was derived
as a function of G. Nevertheless, the assumption of Poisson
distributed number of requests holds only when the number
of nodes goes to infinity and the transmission probability of
each request approaches zero. In [36], the contention among
requests was decoupled from the queueing analysis of data
packets by establishing a Markov process for the requests. The
network throughput was further characterized and maximized

III. SYSTEM MODEL
Consider a slotted Aloha network where n nodes transmit
to a single receiver, each with an identical arrival process of
data packets and equipped with an infinite-size buffer to store
incoming packets. All the nodes are synchronized and can start
a transmission only at the beginning of a time slot.2 Assume
the collision model at the receiver, that is, if more than one
node transmits simultaneously, a collision occurs and all of
them fail. A transmission is successful if and only if there are
no concurrent transmissions.
With connection-based slotted Aloha, nodes would send
requests to establish a connection before their data transmis-
sions. Assume that each node sends a request with probability
q when a batch of M data packets is formed in the buffer.
If a request is successfully received, i.e., an acknowledgement
(ACK) is sent by the receiver, then the node can start trans-
mitting M data packets. Otherwise, it retransmits the request
with probability q in the next time slot.3
As Fig. 1 illustrates, each successful transmission includes
M data packets and the signaling overhead. Without loss of
generality, suppose that the transmission time of each packet
is equal to one time slot. The overhead time of each successful
transmission is denoted as δ, in the unit of time slots, where
one time slot is for connection establishment and the other

In this paper, we are interested in throughput and delay
performance of connection-based slotted Aloha networks. For
each node, as at most one data packet is successfully trans-
mitted in each time slot, the average number of successfully
transmitted data packets per time slot is equal to the fraction
of time for its successful data packet transmission, which is
defined as its data throughput λout. The data throughput of

For each data packet, define its queueing delay T as the
time from its arrival till its successful transmission, in the unit
of time slots. To be more specific, the queueing delay of a
data packet consists of the waiting time in the buffer and the
service time. The waiting time in the buffer includes the time
for forming a batch with M data packets, and the time for
this batch waiting to be the HOL batch. The service time of
a data packet includes the time of its batch contending for
channel access, and the transmission time after a connection
is successfully established.

C. Simulation Results
This section presents the simulation results to verify
the preceding theoretical analysis. We consider an n-node
connection-based slotted Aloha network where each node has
Bernoulli packet arrivals with rate λ. The simulation setting
is identical to the system model described in Section III.
Each simulation is carried out for 108 time slots, and the
initial number of data packets in each node’s buffer is set to
20. In simulations, the steady-state probability of successful
transmission of HOL requests p is obtained by calculating the
ratio of the number of successful requests to the total number
of transmitted requests; the data throughput ˆλout is obtained by
calculating the ratio of the number of successful data packets
to the total number of time slots 108; the mean queueing delay
of data packets T is obtained by calculating the ratio of the
sum of queueing delay of all the arrival data packets to the
total number of arrival data packets.
Section IV has demonstrated that the network operates at
either the unsaturated steady-state point pL or the saturated
steady-state point pA, where pL is invariant with respect to
the transmission probability q and determined by the batch
size M, the aggregate input rate ˆλ and the overhead time
for each successful transmission δ, while pA only depends
on the transmission probability q and the number of nodes n.

unsaturated steady-state point pL varies with the batch size M

q. The saturated steady-state point pA, on the other hand,
is closely dependent of q and independent of M.

B. Case Study: Random Access in 5G Cellular Networks
To demonstrate how to apply the above analysis to practical
networks, let us consider the 2-step RA-SDT and 4-step
RA-SDT schemes specified in 3GPP Release 17 [22], as shown
in Fig 9. Compared to the 4-step RA-SDT scheme, where each
MTD has to first transmit Msg1 to obtain an uplink grant for
Msg3 transmission, the 2-step RA-SDT scheme allows each
MTD to transmit Msg1 and Msg3 in one message, MsgA,
without an uplink grant. Thus, 2-step RA-SDT and 4-step RA-
SDT are corresponding to grant-free random access and grant-
based random access, respectively.
As Fig. 9a illustrates, with grant-free 2-step RA-SDT
scheme, each node transmits its data payload along with the
preamble in MsgA. If the Random Access Response (RAR)
message and the Radio Resource Control (RRC) Release
message are received within the MsgB Response Window, then
the node knows that its MsgA transmission has been success-
ful. With grant-based 4-step RA-SDT scheme, on the other
hand, each node can transmit its data payload in Msg3 only
after sending the preamble and receiving the RAR message
within the Msg2 Response Window, as shown in Fig. 9b. The
overhead parameters of 2-step and 4-step RA-SDT schemes
are summarized in Table I.

VII. DISCUSSIONS
Note that the above analysis is based on a few key assump-
tions. In this section, we will further discuss how to extend
the analysis to incorporate: 1) asymmetric input rates and
transmission probabilities of nodes, 2) finite buffer size, and
3) more advanced receiver design.
A. Asymmetric Scenario
The preceding analysis has focused on the symmetric sce-
nario, where all the nodes have the same data input rate
and transmission probability. With distinct applications and
quality-of-service requirements of nodes, however, the input
rate and transmission probability may vary from node to node.
It is, therefore, of great practical importance to extend the anal-
ysis to the general asymmetric scenario. For connection-free
Aloha, it has been shown in [44] that similar to the analysis for
the symmetric case, the key to characterizing the service rates
of nodes’ queues still lies in: 1) establishing a Markov renewal
process for each node’s HOL packets, and 2) developing the
fixed-point equations of steady-state probabilities of successful
transmission of HOL packets. Different from the symmetric
case, however, different nodes may have distinct steady-state
probabilities of successful transmission of HOL packets, which
need to be jointly solved.
For connection-based Aloha, the analytical framework pro-
posed in this paper for the symmetric scenario can also be
extended to the general asymmetric scenario. Specifically, for
Node i ∈{1, · · · , n}, a Markov renewal process can be

can be analyzed by following a similar approach as presented
in Section V. With nodes having distinct performance, how-
ever, fairness needs to be further taken into consideration when
optimizing the system performance.
B. Finite-Size Buffer
In the preceding analysis, each node is assumed to have an
infinite-size data buffer. In practice, however, the buffer size
is finite, with which arrival data packets would be dropped
if there is no vacancy in the buffer. Intuitively, the optimal
throughput performance is independent of the buffer size, as it
is achieved only in the saturated condition with all nodes’
queues always nonempty, and determined by the contention
of nodes’ HOL requests. The minimum mean queueing delay
of data packets, on the other hand, could be improved by
reducing the buffer size, because for the data packets that
are not dropped, the waiting time in the buffer would be on
average shorter with a smaller buffer size.
To extend the analysis to the finite-buffer-size scenario,
the key still lies in the characterization of service processes
of nodes’ queues, which is determined by the aggregate
activities of HOL requests. Specifically, we can adopt the
methodology proposed in this paper to first model the behavior
of HOL requests and then derive the steady-state probability of
successful transmission of HOL requests. The throughput and
delay performance of each data queue can be further char-
acterized by following the standard finite-capacity queueing
analysis.
C. Advanced Receiver Design
Another key assumption in the preceding analysis is that the
collision model is adopted at the receiver side. It is important
to further incorporate more sophisticated receiver design into
the analysis. To that end, the proposed Markov renewal
process for HOL requests remains unchanged. Yet the fixed-
point equation of the steady-state probability of successful
transmission of HOL requests needs to be re-established, as it
closely depends on the definition of successful transmission
that varies under different assumptions of the receiver type
and channel model. Specifically, (13) can be generalized to

Note that with more sophisticated receivers, the transmis-
sion probability of each node can be jointly tuned with other
system parameters when optimizing the network performance.
For connection-free Aloha, it has been shown in [45] and
[46] that with the capture receiver or SIC receiver, the SINR
threshold is a key parameter that should be jointly tuned with
the transmission probability of each node to maximize the sum
data rate. For connection-based Aloha, the SINR threshold
also determines a fundamental tradeoff between the transmis-
sion time of each request and the probability of successful
transmission of requests: A larger SINR threshold leads to a
higher encoding rate and thus shorter transmission time of a
request, but lower probability of successful transmission of
requests. As a result, by increasing the SINR threshold, the
time slot length σ, which is determined by the transmission
time of a request, can be reduced, yet it takes more time slots
for a request to be successfully transmitted. To optimize the
mean queueing delay of data packets, the SINR threshold and
the transmission probability of each node should be jointly
tuned.
VIII. CONCLUSION
In this paper, the maximum data throughput and the min-
imum mean queueing delay of data packets for connection-
based slotted Aloha are characterized and compared with
those for connection-free slotted Aloha. Based on the proposed
request-queue model, the service rate of each request queue is
derived, from which the data throughput and the mean queue-
ing delay of data packets are further obtained as functions of
transmission probability of nodes q. As the mean queueing
delay of data packets is finite only when the data queue is
unsaturated, the region Sun(q, ˆλ) of transmission probability
q for each queue to stay unsaturated is also characterized. The
analysis shows that to maximize the data throughput or to
minimize the mean queueing delay, the optimal tuning of trans-
mission probability q only requires the long-term statistical
information such as the aggregate data input rate ˆλ. Moreover,
with a larger batch size (or data payload transmission time)
M, the maximum data throughput and unsaturated region
of transmission probability can always be improved. Yet the
minimum mean queueing delay could be increased when the
data input rate is low.

Aloha is further compared to that with connection-free slot-
ted Aloha from the aspects of maximum data throughput,
unsaturated region of transmission probability, and minimum
